The handle has flipped. From outside the panel the story looks the same every time: the circuit is open. Inside a common thermal-magnetic breaker, though, two different mechanisms can release the same latch. One is a strip of two metals that bends when it warms. The other is a small electromagnet that yanks an armature when the current spikes. This page is the theory of those two paths — not a guide to working on live panels, resetting breakers, or choosing ratings.

## Two paths, one latch

ABB describes its electromechanical trip unit as a thermal part that is temperature-sensitive and a magnetic part that is current-sensitive, and states that **both act independently** on the breaker's trip mechanism [[1]](#source-1). NEETS draws the same combined element: a series electromagnet and a bimetal heated by load current, sharing one trip bar [[3]](#source-3). CBI's thermal-magnetic note puts the bimetal behind the trip bar in the current path and the electromagnet-and-armature pair on the short-circuit path [[2]](#source-2).

<Figure1 />
So the decision this page is built around is simple: **which path opened the contacts, and on what time scale?** Sustained overload warms the strip over seconds (or longer). A short circuit builds a field strong enough to pull the armature in a fraction of a cycle [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2)[[3]](#source-3).

## Thermal path: overload and the bimetal

The thermal element is a **bimetal** (two metals bonded together) that sits in the current-carrying path [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2). Current through the strip produces heat. That is the same *P* = *I*²*R* heating in [Ohm's law](/ohms-law/): more current means much more heat in the metal. Double the current and, for the same resistance, you get four times the heating power — which is why a modest rise above rated current can still warm the strip enough to matter if it lasts.

The two metals expand differently as they warm, so the strip bends. As it bends it pushes or pulls the trip bar and releases the latch [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2)[[3]](#source-3). ABB places the bimetal behind the trip bar as part of the breaker's current-carrying path; when overload current heats it, the bend pulls the trip bar and opens the contacts [[1]](#source-1). CBI's description is the same mechanism in different words: the bimetal heats, bends, touches and rotates the trip bar [[2]](#source-2).

ABB and CBI both state that the time needed for the bimetal to bend **varies inversely with the current**: a larger overload trips faster [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2). That inverse-time behaviour is why a mild overload can sit for a long time before the handle moves, while a heavier overload dumps the latch sooner — still on a thermal time scale, not a magnetic one. NEETS compares the thermal element to a delay fuse for the same reason: it protects against a small overload that continues for a long time, and the larger the overload, the faster it trips [[3]](#source-3).

What the thermal path is for, in manufacturer language, is continuous or long-time overcurrent that would otherwise cook conductors and equipment [[1]](#source-1). ABB notes that low-magnitude, long-time overcurrents reduce the life of the installation and, if left unchecked, can lead to fire [[1]](#source-1). What the thermal path is *not* for is an almost-instantaneous short: the strip has not had time to heat [[3]](#source-3).

## Magnetic path: short circuit and the electromagnet

The magnetic element is an **electromagnet in series** with the load and a movable **armature** [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2)[[3]](#source-3). Current in the coil makes a magnetic field. Field strength rises with the current in the coil [[3]](#source-3). At ordinary load the field is too weak to pull the trip bar, and the contacts stay closed [[3]](#source-3). When the current jumps to short-circuit levels, the field rises with it, the armature is attracted toward the magnet, and the trip bar releases the latch [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2)[[3]](#source-3).

CBI describes the armature rotating the trip bar as its top is drawn to the electromagnet [[2]](#source-2). ABB's application guide shows an armature-and-plunger arrangement that pushes a hammer trip against the movable contact so the contacts open [[1]](#source-1). The mechanical details differ by product line; the physics is the same: enough ampere-turns, and the armature moves.

That action is meant to be **near-instantaneous**. ABB's application guide puts the contact opening on a short circuit on the order of half a millisecond for the current-limiting example it describes [[1]](#source-1). ABB SACE's trip-curve note places a typical magnetic trip in some tens of milliseconds [[4]](#source-4). Those numbers are design examples, not a universal constant. NEETS simply says the magnetic element trips as soon as the preset current is present, even when the thermal element has not had time to react [[3]](#source-3). On some breakers the gap between the trip bar and the magnet (and therefore the trip current) is adjustable [[3]](#source-3); this page does not cover how to set that.

## Comparison: which path, when

| | Thermal path | Magnetic path |
|---|---|---|
| What it senses | Heating from sustained current in the bimetal [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2) | Magnetic field from high current in the series coil [[1]](#source-1)[[3]](#source-3) |
| Time scale | Inverse-time: seconds (or more) at modest overload; faster as current rises [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2) | Near-instant (milliseconds) [[1]](#source-1)[[3]](#source-3)[[4]](#source-4) |
| Physical part | Bimetal strip in the current path [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2) | Electromagnet + armature [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2)[[3]](#source-3) |
| Typical fault it catches | Overload that lasts long enough to heat the strip [[1]](#source-1) | Short circuit / very high current [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2) |
| What it does not catch alone | An almost-instant short (strip has not heated) [[3]](#source-3) | A mild, long overload below the magnetic threshold [[1]](#source-1)[[3]](#source-3) |

A **thermal-magnetic** breaker is the common combined unit: both elements on one latch, acting independently [[1]](#source-1)[[3]](#source-3). Magnetic-only and other variants exist (CBI notes magnetic-only units for some motor short-circuit roles [[2]](#source-2)); they are named here only so the comparison stays honest.


Together, the two paths cover the two overcurrent stories that matter for theory: heat that builds over time, and current that spikes at once [[1]](#source-1)[[3]](#source-3). A single handle hides which path fired; reading the time scale in a practice prompt is how you tell them apart (original tip).

<Predict question="A branch carries a modest overload for several minutes, then the handle flips. Which path most likely released the latch, and why?">
The thermal path. Minutes at modest overcurrent is the bimetal's inverse-time world: the strip had time to heat and bend [[1]](#source-1)[[2]](#source-2). The magnetic armature needs a much higher current and acts in milliseconds [[1]](#source-1)[[3]](#source-3). (Original practice prompt.)

## Electronic trip units (named only)

Some low-voltage breakers may use an **electronic** trip unit: a microprocessor that processes the current signal and commands the opening, instead of a bimetal-plus-electromagnet pair [[4]](#source-4). ABB SACE contrasts the two families directly: thermomagnetic units use a bimetal for overload and an electromagnetic device for short circuit; electronic units process the current digitally and can offer long-time, short-time, instantaneous and ground-fault functions [[4]](#source-4). Naming that family keeps the thermal-versus-magnetic comparison from pretending it is the only technology in a panel. This page still stays on thermal-magnetic theory. No product picks, no setting guides, no coordination studies.

## Inrush is not the same as overload

A motor or transformer can draw a large current for a cycle or two when it is first energised (inrush — original theory note). That spike is short. The thermal strip barely warms; the magnetic element is the one that might see it. On published curves the magnetic threshold is set as a multiple of rated current *I*<sub>n</sub> [[4]](#source-4). Keeping that multiple above ordinary inrush so starts do not nuisance-trip is original theory reasoning, not a claim from the SACE note. Inductive loads also oppose sudden current change — the idea behind [AC impedance](/ac-impedance/) — but the breaker still only "knows" the current through its own path. Selecting a trip curve for inrush is a design task; it is out of scope here.

Nothing on this page is a procedure for a live panel. It does not tell you how to reset a breaker, how to lock out a panel, how to measure a fault, or which ampere frame to buy.
