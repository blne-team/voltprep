One hundred questions, four hours, and 70% to pass [[1]](#source-1) [[6]](#source-6). Those three numbers are the whole format of the Red Seal Construction Electrician exam, and each one comes from the Red Seal Program itself: the trade's exam page gives the 100 questions [[1]](#source-1), and the program's preparation guide gives the four-hour writing time and the 70% pass mark [[6]](#source-6). What the official pages don't do is tell you what those numbers mean for your study plan. This page lays out the official breakdown, shows where the electrical theory sits inside it, and ends with original study advice for turning the weights into hours.

These are independent study notes. Fieldbook is not affiliated with or endorsed by the Red Seal Program, the Canadian Council of Directors of Apprenticeship (CCDA), Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC) or Skilled Trades Ontario. Every number below links to the official page it came from, checked on 2026-10-12 [[1]](#source-1) [[6]](#source-6). Nothing here reproduces exam questions or electrical code rules.

## The format in numbers

The exam is 100 multiple-choice questions [[1]](#source-1). Each question has four options with exactly one correct answer, and the Red Seal Program says the questions are not trick questions, though the wrong options are not always obvious [[1]](#source-1).

The writing time is four hours and the pass mark is 70% [[6]](#source-6). The 70% figure appears again in the trade's self-assessment guide [[7]](#source-7). Provincial authorities that run the sittings say the same about time. SkilledTradesBC lists the time limit for interprovincial Red Seal exams as 4 hours [[8]](#source-8), and Skilled Trades Ontario's June 2026 exam preparation guide lists a 4-hour limit and a 70% passing grade [[9]](#source-9). The Ontario guide gives a range of 90 to 150 questions, but that range covers every trade it certifies [[9]](#source-9); for construction electricians the Red Seal page says 100 [[1]](#source-1).

The Red Seal guide suggests dividing 240 minutes by the number of questions to get your average time per question [[6]](#source-6). For this exam that works out to 2.4 minutes each [[1]](#source-1) [[6]](#source-6). It is a useful number to carry into practice sessions, because a calculation question can easily eat three or four minutes and has to be paid for by quicker recall questions elsewhere.

The trade's formula list is provided at the sitting, so you don't need to memorize it [[1]](#source-1). In British Columbia, personal calculators and code books are not allowed in the room and the authority supplies a calculator and any required code book [[8]](#source-8). Other provinces set their own exam-room rules, so check with yours.

## Which version of the exam you'll write

This matters more than it sounds. The Red Seal trade page lists two occupational standards. The newer Red Seal Occupational Standard (RSOS), dated 2025, is labelled as the most recent, with exams based on it "currently under development" [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4). The previous standard, dated 2021, is the one the page says is used as the basis for current exams [[3]](#source-3) [[5]](#source-5).

The two standards weight the major work activities (MWAs) differently:

| MWA | 2021 RSOS (current exams) | 2025 RSOS (new exams, in development) |
|---|---|---|
| A Performs common occupational skills | 11% [[5]](#source-5) | 12% [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4) |
| B Generating, distribution and service systems | 28% [[5]](#source-5) | 26% [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4) |
| C Wiring systems | 31% [[5]](#source-5) | 34% [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4) |
| D Motors and control systems | 20% [[5]](#source-5) | 18% [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4) |
| E Signalling and communication systems | 10% [[5]](#source-5) | 10% [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4) |

The Examination Weightings page on red-seal.ca shows the 2025 figures [[2]](#source-2). The question counts on the Exam Information page (below) sit close to the 2021 percentages but are not identical: 30 questions for C and 21 for D, against 31% and 20% in the 2021 standard [[1]](#source-1) [[5]](#source-5). The Red Seal Program doesn't explain the gap on either page. As of 2026-10-12, the safest reading is that you'll most likely write an exam built on the 2021 standard, and that wiring systems will gain weight when the 2025 exams arrive [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4) [[5]](#source-5). Check the trade page again before you book [[3]](#source-3).

Two things stand out. First, wiring systems (C) and distribution (B) together hold 58 of the 100 questions [[1]](#source-1). Second, just three tasks, C-16, C-17 and D-22, hold 26 questions between them, more than a quarter of the exam [[1]](#source-1). A task with one or two questions still matters, but it can't rescue a weak result in the big ones.

It helps to know what sits inside those big tasks. The weightings page lists the sub-tasks [[2]](#source-2). Under C-16 you'll find installing conductors and cables, conduit and fittings, raceways, and boxes and enclosures, plus servicing them [[2]](#source-2). C-17 covers luminaires, wiring devices, lighting controls and lighting standards, servicing branch circuitry, and two specialised systems: airport visual aids and traffic signals [[2]](#source-2). D-22 splits into installing and servicing motor starters and motor control devices [[2]](#source-2). Reading those lists against your own work history shows quickly where your experience is thin. An apprentice who has spent years on commercial lighting may know C-17 well and still need to build up starters and controls from the training material.

Only sub-tasks that are part of the trade's common core can be tested. The preparation guide explains that a sub-task joins the common core when at least 70% of provinces and territories say journeypersons perform it [[6]](#source-6). The RSOS marks which sub-tasks those are, which is one more reason to read the standard your exam is built on [[5]](#source-5).

## What kind of questions to expect

Red Seal sorts its questions into three types and gives a range for each in this trade [[1]](#source-1):

- **Knowledge and recall**: definitions, facts and principles, between 5% and 15% of questions [[1]](#source-1).
- **Procedural and application**: applying procedures to a situation, between 60% and 70% [[1]](#source-1).
- **Critical thinking**: interpreting data, solving problems and reaching conclusions, between 20% and 30% [[1]](#source-1).

So most of the exam asks you to apply what you know to a described situation, and a fifth to almost a third asks you to work something out [[1]](#source-1). Pure recall is the smallest slice [[1]](#source-1). The official page shows one example of each type and links to the program's sample questions [[1]](#source-1). Use those to see the style of question. This page doesn't copy or paraphrase them.

## Where the theory sits

The exam tests a trade, not a physics course, but a handful of relationships run under many of the tasks. The trade's official formula list is the clearest guide to which ones, because it is the set the Red Seal Program expects you might need [[1]](#source-1). It includes current from voltage and resistance, power as voltage times current and as current squared times resistance, power factor as true power over apparent power, three-phase power and kVA with the √3 factor, the line and phase relationships, the transformer turns ratio, short-circuit current from a transformer's percent impedance, motor frequency from poles and speed, and horsepower to watts [[1]](#source-1).

Here is how those relationships map onto the breakdown. This mapping is original study advice, built from the task names and the formula list; it is not an official Red Seal statement.

**Circuit basics across B and C.** Branch circuits, conductors and protection devices (B-8, C-16, C-17) all lean on the link between voltage, current and resistance, and on power turned into heat in a conductor [[1]](#source-1). If those feel shaky, start with [Ohm's law](/ohms-law/): it covers *I* = *V* ÷ *R* and the *P* = *I*² × *R* heating idea with a live explainer.

**Transformers (B-15, 4 questions).** The formula list gives the turns ratio linking primary and secondary turns, voltages and currents, and the short-circuit current from percent impedance [[1]](#source-1). Being able to move between those ratios in either direction, without a formula sheet open, is the theory core of the task.

**Three-phase systems (B-7, B-9, D-24).** Three-phase services, distribution equipment and motors all use line and phase values and the √3 factor in power and kVA [[1]](#source-1). The [three-phase power calculator](/tools/three-phase-power/) lets you check your arithmetic and see how power factor changes real power.

**AC behaviour and power factor.** Power factor appears on the formula list as true power over apparent power [[1]](#source-1). Behind it sits the idea that inductive loads such as motors shift current out of step with voltage; the [AC impedance guide](/ac-impedance/) explains that phase angle. The [series RLC circuit calculator](/tools/series-rlc/) shows that shift with the formulas in view.

**Motors and drives (D-23, D-24).** Synchronous speed comes from frequency and the number of poles, and the formula list gives that relationship together with the horsepower-to-watts conversion [[1]](#source-1). Those two cover most of the motor arithmetic you'll practise.

None of this replaces knowing installation practice and the code your jurisdiction uses. The code and the procedures sit with your training program and your provincial authority, not with a study site.

## Ontario candidates

In Ontario the trade is called Electrician – Construction and Maintenance (309A), and Skilled Trades Ontario says it is part of the Red Seal program under the national title Construction Electrician [[10]](#source-10). Passing the certifying exam leads to a Certificate of Qualification from Skilled Trades Ontario [[10]](#source-10). Ontario's exam guide confirms the same four-hour limit and 70% passing grade [[9]](#source-9). [309A vs 442A](/309a-vs-442a/) compares 309A with the industrial electrician trade (442A) [[10]](#source-10).

Booking, fees, eligibility and rewrite rules are set by each province and territory, not by the Red Seal Program [[6]](#source-6). Check them with your own authority: [Skilled Trades Ontario](https://www.skilledtradesontario.ca/trade-information/electrician-construction-and-maintenance/) and [SkilledTradesBC](https://skilledtradesbc.ca/what-to-expect-on-exam-day) are two examples. This page doesn't summarize their rules.

## Turning the weights into a study plan

Everything in this section is original study advice, not guidance from the Red Seal Program or any authority.

**Split your hours by question count.** The simplest plan that respects the exam is to give each MWA a share of your study time equal to its share of the 100 questions [[1]](#source-1). With 60 hours to spend, that is roughly 7 hours for A, 17 for B, 18 for C, 13 for D and 6 for E [[1]](#source-1). Then shift time toward the tasks where you rate yourself weakest. The official self-assessment guide is built for exactly this: rate yourself task by task, then look at the areas where you have the most to gain [[7]](#source-7).

**Know which standard you are studying.** Read the RSOS your exam is based on, not just the one with the newest date [[3]](#source-3). The task names and sub-tasks are the syllabus.

**Practise the arithmetic the way you'll do it in the room.** Use a basic scientific calculator of the kind your province supplies or allows, and work from the formula list rather than your own notes, since that is what you'll have on the day [[1]](#source-1) [[8]](#source-8). Practise rearranging each formula for every quantity in it. Critical-thinking questions make up a fifth to almost a third of the exam [[1]](#source-1), and many involve a calculation.

**Budget time per question.** At 2.4 minutes per question on average [[1]](#source-1) [[6]](#source-6), set a pace in practice: answer the ones you know, mark the long calculations, and come back. The Red Seal guide recommends this kind of time check [[6]](#source-6).

**Read for what's asked.** The wrong options are designed to be plausible [[1]](#source-1). Before choosing, put the question into your own words: which quantity, what units, which situation. Many wrong answers come from answering a slightly different question.

**Use only official practice material as a model of the exam.** The Red Seal sample questions and the self-assessment guide are the official tools [[1]](#source-1) [[7]](#source-7). Commercial question banks vary, and their claims about matching the real exam can't be checked against anything official.
