Picture two electricians in Ontario. One is pulling cable through a new condo tower, setting panels and wiring suites floor by floor. The other works in a food plant, tracing a fault in a motor drive before the line restarts. The first is likely working under a 309A certificate, Electrician – Construction and Maintenance, and the second under a 442A certificate, Industrial Electrician [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). The two trades overlap a lot in training, but they differ in one way that matters every working day: 309A is compulsory and 442A is not [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2).

These are independent study notes. Fieldbook is not affiliated with Skilled Trades Ontario, the Red Seal Program or the Electrical Safety Authority (ESA). Every fact below links to the official page it came from, checked on 2026-10-12 [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). Nothing here is legal advice about who may do what work.

The training-hours rows disagree, and that isn't a typo on this page. The next section explains it.

## What each trade covers

Skilled Trades Ontario's 309A description runs from planning and installing to inspecting, verifying and keeping electrical systems working, in homes, commercial and institutional buildings, and industry [[1]](#source-1). Those systems include heating, lighting, power, communication, control, security, and renewable energy and storage [[1]](#source-1). The 2025 trade report is shorter: it talks about the wiring, fixtures and controls inside buildings and other structures [[3]](#source-3).

The 442A description leans toward plant equipment [[2]](#source-2). It lists industrial wiring and control equipment along with motors, generators, distribution equipment, motor drives, stand-by power, instrumentation and similar systems, and it calls out programming, calibration and preventive or predictive maintenance by name [[2]](#source-2). The trade report sums it up as industrial electrical equipment and its electrical and electronic controls [[4]](#source-4).

So both trades can find themselves in an industrial building. The 309A description reaches across every kind of building, while the 442A description goes deeper into running equipment: drives, controls, instruments and their upkeep [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2).

## Training hours: two figures, and which one is current

Skilled Trades Ontario publishes two different sets of hours for both trades, and as of 2026-10-12 both are still online [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3).

The trade information pages say 9,000 hours, made up of 8,160 hours on the job and 840 hours in school, with three levels of in-school theory [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). For 309A the page adds that the Registrar set those hours under the Building Opportunities in the Skilled Trades Act, 2021 [[1]](#source-1).

The 2025 trade reports for both trades give 7,950 hours on the job and 1,050 hours of technical training across four levels [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4). That second figure lines up with the new electrical trades curriculum. The curriculum standard sets 270 hours each for levels 1, 2 and 3, and 240 hours for each trade's level 4 [[5]](#source-5). Added up, that is 1,050 in-school hours, the same total the trade reports give [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4) [[5]](#source-5).

The new curriculum is being phased in: level 1 from September 1, 2024, level 2 from September 1, 2025, and levels 3 and 4 from September 1, 2026 [[2]](#source-2) [[6]](#source-6). Apprentices who took the new level 1 on or after September 1, 2024 must take the new later levels, including level 4 [[6]](#source-6). Apprentices registered earlier who complete level 3 of the old curriculum by September 1, 2026 aren't required to take the new level 4 [[6]](#source-6).

The reading that fits both documents: the 7,950 + 1,050 figure, over four levels, describes the new program, and the 9,000-hour, three-level figure describes the 2003 program that is being retired [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4) [[5]](#source-5) [[6]](#source-6). The trade information pages hadn't been updated to match as of 2026-10-12 [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). If your hours matter to a decision, ask Skilled Trades Ontario which program your training agreement falls under.

## What the two trades share in school

The new curriculum standard covers 309A, 442A and the domestic and rural trade (309C) together [[5]](#source-5). Levels 1 to 3 are the same common core for all three of those trades [[5]](#source-5). Only the fourth level differs. The 309A and 442A versions of level 4 share five common core subjects, and the rest is trade-specific: two subjects for 309A and three for 442A [[5]](#source-5).

The shared level 4 subjects are building automation systems, professionalism and ethics, power conditioning, advanced motors and generators, and high-voltage service and operation [[5]](#source-5). The 442A-only subjects are advanced instrumentation, pneumatic and hydraulic systems, and automated control systems [[5]](#source-5). The 309A-only subjects are specialty installations and a fourth code course [[5]](#source-5).

For a learner, that means the circuit theory is the same for both trades. Ohm's law, power, AC behaviour, transformers and three-phase systems all live in the shared levels. If you want to firm up the basics, [Ohm's law](/ohms-law/) is the place to start on this site. The industrial level 4 builds on motors, controls and instruments [[5]](#source-5), so three-phase arithmetic gets regular use; the [three-phase power calculator](/tools/three-phase-power/) is a quick way to check it.

## The exam and the Red Seal

Both trades end in a certifying exam, and passing it leads Skilled Trades Ontario to issue a Certificate of Qualification (C of Q) [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). Both trades are part of the interprovincial Red Seal Program, 309A under the national title Construction Electrician and 442A under Industrial Electrician [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). The Red Seal Program's own Construction Electrician page lists Ontario's title, Electrician – Construction and Maintenance, as the same trade [[8]](#source-8).

Skilled Trades Ontario's exam preparation guide lists a four-hour time limit and a 70% passing grade for its exams, and names both Red Seal and provincial C of Q exams [[9]](#source-9). Because they are separate Red Seal trades, each has its own exam built on its own national standard. For a look at what the construction electrician exam covers, see the separate breakdown post in this series.

## A C of Q is not an ESA licence

This is the mix-up that comes up most. A 309A or 442A Certificate of Qualification is a trade certificate from Skilled Trades Ontario [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). It is not a licence from the Electrical Safety Authority.

ESA runs its own licences, including the Master Electrician licence. Passing the Master Electrician exam is one of the requirements for that licence, and the exam is offered only through ESA [[7]](#source-7). According to ESA, the exam covers worker-safety rules, running a business, and the whole of the Ontario Electrical Safety Code [[7]](#source-7). ESA also licenses electrical contractors [[7]](#source-7). For what each licence allows and who is eligible, go to ESA directly; this page doesn't summarize those rules.

So when someone says "309A licence", they usually mean the C of Q. It is a certificate in the trade, and for 309A it has to be kept renewed [[1]](#source-1).

## Where 309C fits

A third Ontario electrical trade often appears next to these two: Electrician – Domestic and Rural (309C) [[5]](#source-5). It uses the same curriculum standard and takes the same first three in-school levels as 309A and 442A, but it has no fourth level [[5]](#source-5). Apprentices in 309C only complete levels 1 to 3 [[5]](#source-5). If you see 309C in a job posting, it is a separate trade, not a step on the way to 309A [[5]](#source-5).

## If you're partway through an apprenticeship

The curriculum change matters most to people already in training. Skilled Trades Ontario's implementation summary sets out who stays on the old program and who moves to the new one [[6]](#source-6). Anyone who takes the new level 1 on or after September 1, 2024 continues on the new levels, including level 4 for 309A and 442A, and has to wait for each new level's start date [[6]](#source-6). Apprentices registered before that date who had finished the old level 1 could carry on with the old levels 2 and 3, provided each was finished by its deadline: September 1, 2025 for level 2 and September 1, 2026 for level 3 [[6]](#source-6). Finish the old level 3 in time and the new level 4 isn't required [[6]](#source-6). Anyone registered earlier who hadn't finished the old level 1 moves onto the full new program [[6]](#source-6).

If you aren't sure which side of those dates you fall on, your training delivery agent or Skilled Trades Ontario can tell you. The summary is detailed, and an apprentice's own record decides it [[6]](#source-6).

## Experienced workers without an Ontario apprenticeship

If you already work in the trade but didn't apprentice in Ontario, both trade reports point to the Trade Equivalency Assessment process for workers with equivalent qualifications or experience [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4). Skilled Trades Ontario runs it; check its page for current requirements.

## Choosing between them

Everything in this section is original study advice, not guidance from Skilled Trades Ontario or any other authority.

**Start from the work you want to do.** If you picture yourself on new builds and renovations across many kinds of buildings, the 309A description fits [[1]](#source-1). If you'd rather keep a plant's equipment running and like controls, drives and instruments, the 442A description fits [[2]](#source-2). Read both official descriptions slowly, because the job titles alone don't tell you much.

**Look at who will sponsor you.** An apprenticeship needs a sponsor and on-the-job training signed off against the trade's training standard [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). The kind of employer you can find (an electrical contractor or a manufacturer with a maintenance department) often decides the trade more than preference does.

**Know what compulsory means for you.** For 309A, the training agreement or certificate and the public register entry are conditions for practising the trade legally in Ontario [[1]](#source-1). For 442A they are not [[2]](#source-2). That difference affects renewals and how employers check credentials. It doesn't make one trade harder than the other.

**Check the entry requirements.** The 2025 trade reports list different academic entry grades: grade 10 for 309A and grade 12 for 442A [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4). If you're still in school, that alone can shape the timing.

**Remember the overlap.** With the first three in-school levels shared, the early theory serves both paths [[5]](#source-5). What you learn about circuits, power and motors carries over whichever you choose.
