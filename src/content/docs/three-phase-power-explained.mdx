Open the panel in a typical Canadian house and the service is single phase, 120/240 V [[2]](#source-2). Walk into a small commercial building and the nameplate on the panel often reads 120/208 V, three phase, wye [[2]](#source-2). Both voltage pairs appear in the supply-voltage table Hydro-Québec publishes under CSA CAN3-C235-83, alongside a three-phase 347/600 V wye service [[2]](#source-2). The second pair is the puzzle: if each hot conductor is 120 V to neutral in both buildings, why is hot-to-hot 240 V in the house and 208 V in the shop [[2]](#source-2)?

The answer is the number √3 [[5]](#source-5), and it shows up again in the power formula. This page explains what three-phase power is, how it compares with single phase, and where that factor comes from. It is theory only: it doesn't cover sizing, protection, connection procedures or code requirements. The worked numbers are original Fieldbook examples that use ideal, balanced parts.

## Single phase vs three phase at a glance

The comparison most readers need is between the two services they will actually meet. The rows below are explained, with sources, in the sections that follow.

| | Single phase (120/240 V) | Three phase wye (120/208 V or 347/600 V) |
|---|---|---|
| Supply voltages in Canada | 120/240 V [[2]](#source-2) | 120/208 V and 347/600 V wye, plus 600 V three-wire [[2]](#source-2) |
| Waveforms | One voltage waveform, at 60 Hz in Hydro-Québec territory [[3]](#source-3) | Three equal waveforms spaced a third of a cycle apart [[4]](#source-4) |
| Hot-to-hot voltage | Twice the hot-to-neutral voltage | √3 times the hot-to-neutral voltage [[5]](#source-5) |
| Instantaneous power | Pulses at twice the line frequency [[4]](#source-4) | Constant for a balanced load [[4]](#source-4) |
| Power formula | *P* = *V*·*I*·cos φ | *P* = √3·*V*<sub>L</sub>·*I*<sub>L</sub>·cos φ (derived below) |
| Conductor material | Reference case | Under the stated assumptions, three-quarters of the material (derived below) |
| Motors | No rotating field from one winding set alone (not covered here) | A polyphase stator winding produces a rotating field [[6]](#source-6) |

## Three voltages, a third of a cycle apart

A balanced three-phase source is three AC voltages of the same size and frequency, each shifted by a third of a cycle, or 120°, from the next [[4]](#source-4). At 60 Hz [[3]](#source-3) one cycle lasts 1/60 of a second, so the three peaks arrive about 5.6 ms apart (original arithmetic).

The useful property is that the three waves always add to zero. Kirtley's notes give it as an identity: cos *x* + cos(*x* − 2π/3) + cos(*x* + 2π/3) = 0 for any *x* [[4]](#source-4). Three equal currents into a balanced wye load therefore cancel at the star point, which is why a balanced load behaves the same whether or not the star point is tied to the source neutral [[4]](#source-4). If the three load impedances aren't equal, the load is unbalanced, and an ungrounded star point no longer sits at zero voltage [[4]](#source-4).

<Predict question="A balanced wye load is disconnected from the neutral but stays on all three lines. Does it keep working the same way?">

In theory, yes. The three equal currents are a third of a cycle apart, so they add to zero at the star point, and no current needs the neutral [[4]](#source-4). That stops being true once the three loads are unequal: the star point then drifts away from zero volts [[4]](#source-4). (Original practice prompt.)

</Predict>

## Where √3 comes from

Draw each line-to-neutral voltage as an arrow (a phasor) from a common centre. The three arrows are the same length and 120° apart [[4]](#source-4). The line-to-line voltage between two hot conductors is the difference between two of those arrows, which is the straight gap from one tip to the other.

<Figure1 />
That gap is the third side of a triangle whose other two sides are equal and meet at 120° [[4]](#source-4). Using the law of cosines with each side equal to *V*, the gap is √(*V*² + *V*² − 2*V*²·cos 120°) = √(3*V*²) = √3·*V*, which matches the factor Kirtley gives [[5]](#source-5). Kirtley also notes that line-to-line voltages are larger than line-to-neutral voltages by √3 and are shifted 30° ahead of them [[5]](#source-5).

So *V*<sub>LL</sub> = √3·*V*<sub>LN</sub> [[5]](#source-5). Check it against the two wye services in the Canadian table [[2]](#source-2):

- 120 V × √3 ≈ 207.8 V, listed as 208 V.
- 347 V × √3 ≈ 601 V, listed as 600 V.

The nominal values are rounded, so the match isn't exact to the volt (original arithmetic). The house service is different because its two hot conductors are not 120° apart [[4]](#source-4). A single-phase service of this kind is one winding with its midpoint as the neutral, so its two halves add straight to double the hot-to-neutral voltage. That description of a split winding is the common textbook model, written here as theory rather than tied to a fetched utility document.

<Predict question="A wye service has 120 V from each phase to neutral. What is the voltage phase to phase: 240 V or something else?">

About 208 V. The two phase voltages are 120° apart, so the gap between them is 120 V × √3 ≈ 207.8 V [[5]](#source-5). You'd only get 240 V if the two voltages were directly opposite each other, as the two halves of a split single-phase service are. (Original practice prompt.)

</Predict>

## Wye and delta: where the √3 lands

There are two ways to connect three loads, or three source windings, to three lines. In wye (star), each element sits between a line and the common star point. In delta, each element sits between two lines. A wye can have four terminals, the three lines plus the star point, while a delta has only three [[5]](#source-5).

- In wye, each element sees the line-to-neutral voltage and carries the line current. The √3 sits on voltage: *V*<sub>LL</sub> = √3·*V*<sub>ph</sub> and *I*<sub>L</sub> = *I*<sub>ph</sub>.
- In delta, each element sees the full line-to-line voltage, and each line current is the difference of two element currents that are 120° apart. That is the same geometry as the voltage triangle above, so the √3 sits on current: *V*<sub>LL</sub> = *V*<sub>ph</sub> and *I*<sub>L</sub> = √3·*I*<sub>ph</sub> (original derivation from the same triangle).

The practical choice this creates is between element ratings. Kirtley works it through for a 6 kW resistive heater built from three elements on a 120/208 V system [[5]](#source-5). Each element delivers 2 kW, so the wye version needs elements of 120² ÷ 2000 = 7.2 Ω and the delta version needs 208² ÷ 2000 = 21.6 Ω [[5]](#source-5). Same heat, same three lines, different elements. This page doesn't describe how to make either connection.

## Power: from three single phases to √3·V·I·cos φ

Look at one phase on its own and its instantaneous power isn't steady. Kirtley's expression for one phase of a balanced load has a term that swings at twice the line frequency, so the power flow pulses, and the same is true of any single-phase load [[4]](#source-4). Add the three phases and the swinging terms cancel, by the same identity as before, leaving a constant total [[4]](#source-4). For a balanced three-phase load, the power delivered doesn't pulse at all.

That constant total is three times the average power of one phase. Kirtley writes it with peak values as (3/2)·|*V*|²/|*Z*|·cos ψ [[4]](#source-4). With RMS values, where each peak is √2 times the RMS value, it becomes *P* = 3·*V*<sub>ph</sub>·*I*<sub>ph</sub>·cos φ, where φ is the angle between each element's voltage and current (original rewriting of [[4]](#source-4)).

Now substitute the line quantities:

- Wye: *V*<sub>ph</sub> = *V*<sub>LL</sub> ÷ √3 and *I*<sub>ph</sub> = *I*<sub>L</sub>, so *P* = 3·(*V*<sub>LL</sub> ÷ √3)·*I*<sub>L</sub>·cos φ = √3·*V*<sub>LL</sub>·*I*<sub>L</sub>·cos φ.
- Delta: *V*<sub>ph</sub> = *V*<sub>LL</sub> and *I*<sub>ph</sub> = *I*<sub>L</sub> ÷ √3, so *P* = 3·*V*<sub>LL</sub>·(*I*<sub>L</sub> ÷ √3)·cos φ = √3·*V*<sub>LL</sub>·*I*<sub>L</sub>·cos φ.

Both connections give the same formula in line quantities, which is why nameplates and calculators can use line voltage and line current without asking which connection is inside (original derivation).

The same √3 carries into the other two power quantities [[5]](#source-5). Apparent power is |*S*| = √3·*V*<sub>LL</sub>·*I*<sub>L</sub> in volt-amperes (VA), and reactive power is *Q* = |*S*|·sin φ in var [[5]](#source-5). In the SI Brochure, var is simply another name for the volt-ampere, kept for reactive power, and it is written in lowercase [[1]](#source-1).

## A worked example you can repeat

Take a balanced load on 600 V line-to-line, drawing 40 A per line at a power factor of 0.85 lagging (original example; 600 V is one of the Canadian three-phase supply voltages [[2]](#source-2)). These are also the default inputs of the [three-phase power calculator](/tools/three-phase-power/), so you can check each step there.

- Apparent power: |*S*| = √3 × 600 V × 40 A ≈ 41 569 VA, or about 41.6 kVA.
- Real power: *P* = 41 569 VA × 0.85 ≈ 35 334 W, or about 35.3 kW.
- Angle: φ = arccos 0.85 ≈ 31.8°.
- Reactive power: *Q* = 41 569 VA × sin 31.8° ≈ 21 898 var, or about 21.9 kvar.

Cross-check it per phase. On a wye supply, each phase sees 600 V ÷ √3 ≈ 346.4 V to neutral [[5]](#source-5), so three phases give 3 × 346.4 V × 40 A × 0.85 ≈ 35.3 kW, the same answer (original arithmetic). The calculator shows results to five significant figures, so expect 41.569 kVA, 35.334 kW and 21.898 kvar.

<Predict question="Keep 600 V and a 0.85 power factor, but halve the line current to 20 A. What happens to real power?">

It halves too, to about 17.7 kW: √3 × 600 V × 20 A × 0.85 ≈ 17 667 W. Power is proportional to line current when voltage and power factor stay fixed. The calculator should show 17.667 kW. (Original practice prompt.)

</Predict>

## The real decision: why three phase at all

For a reader comparing the two services, the question is what three phase buys. Two answers follow from the theory above, and one comparison needs its assumptions stated.

**Steady power.** A balanced three-phase load takes constant instantaneous power, while a single-phase load takes power that pulses at twice the line frequency [[4]](#source-4).

**A rotating field.** A polyphase stator winding fed with balanced voltages sets up a magnetic field that travels around the machine. That field induces currents in the rotor, and those currents interact with it to make torque [[6]](#source-6). This page doesn't cover how single-phase motors start.

**Less conductor material, under stated assumptions.** Compare a single-phase two-wire line with a three-phase three-wire line. Assume the same delivered power, the same voltage between conductors, the same length, unity power factor, the same total resistive loss, and conductor resistance inversely proportional to cross-section.

- Single phase: power is *V*·*I*, so two conductors each carry *I*, and the loss is 2·*I*²·*R*<sub>1</sub>.
- Three phase: power is √3·*V*·*I*<sub>L</sub>, so for the same power each line carries *I* ÷ √3, and the loss is 3·(*I* ÷ √3)²·*R*<sub>3</sub> = *I*²·*R*<sub>3</sub>.
- Equal loss means *R*<sub>3</sub> = 2·*R*<sub>1</sub>, so each three-phase conductor needs half the cross-section of a single-phase one.
- Material: three conductors at half the size is 1.5 single-phase conductors' worth, against 2 for single phase, which is three-quarters as much.

That result is an original derivation from the formulas on this page. It holds only under those assumptions, it ignores the neutral of a four-wire wye, and it is not a sizing rule. Nor does it say three phase is "three times the power" of single phase; with the same conductors and currents the comparison depends on what is held equal.
