Use this electrical learning path to connect theory, representations, systems, and trade context instead of collecting isolated facts. It is **original study advice**, not electrical-code guidance or field instruction.

:::caution[Know the scope]
Electrical work can be hazardous and is regulated by provinces and territories. Learn practical work through your apprenticeship program, employer, qualified instructors, and the rules in force where you work.
:::

## Build the model first

Start with a small set of ideas that explain many later topics:

1. Describe charge, electric potential difference, current, resistance, energy, and power in words.
2. Show the same relationship with a sketch or schematic.
3. Express it mathematically and keep the units attached.
4. Explain what a measurement could tell you about the model.

The goal is not to memorize four separate versions. It is to move between them without losing the underlying idea.

### Try it with one circuit

Ohm's law is a good first model because each view is small enough to hold in your head at once. Change the source voltage or the resistance and watch the words, sketch, maths and meter move together.

<Predict question="Keep the source at 12 V and double the resistance from 24 Ω to 48 Ω. What happens to the current and to the power turned into heat?">

Both halve. The current falls from 0.5 A to 0.25 A because *I* = *V* ÷ *R*, and the power falls from 6 W to 3 W because *P* = *V* × *I* with the same voltage. Now set the resistance back to 24 Ω and double the voltage instead: the current doubles, but the power goes up four times. Check it with the sliders, then explain in your own words why the two changes behave differently.

## Learn in connected layers

### Foundations

Build a clear picture of quantities, units, circuit relationships, energy transfer, magnetism, and electromagnetic induction.

### Representations

Practise reading plain-language descriptions, schematics, one-line diagrams, tables, graphs, formulas, and instrument indications as different views of the same system.

### Systems

Organize new material around systems such as sources, distribution, loads, control, protection, communication, and building automation. Ask what each part does, what information flows through it, and how it relates to the whole.

### Trade context

Connect the concept to terminology, drawings, tools, safety responsibilities, and the tasks named in the current occupational standard. Keep procedural work inside qualified training and supervised practice.

## Close the learning loop

For each study session:

1. **Retrieve:** explain the idea before reopening your notes.
2. **Represent:** draw it, label it, and write the relevant relationship.
3. **Apply:** work through an original example or a source-backed practice question.
4. **Check:** compare your reasoning with a reliable source, not just the final answer.
5. **Record:** keep an error log that names the misconception and the correction.

## Choose sources in order

Prefer sources closest to the decision you are trying to make:

- your provincial or territorial apprenticeship and certification authority for eligibility, training, and local requirements;
- the code edition and amendments adopted in your jurisdiction for code-specific study;
- manufacturer documentation for a specific product or system; and
- qualified instruction and supervised workplace experience for practical procedures.

Record the title, publisher, date or edition, jurisdiction, and link with every important note. That makes the note easier to verify and safer to share.
